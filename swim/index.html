Swim · IP68 · 2 m

Listen while you swim

Laps do not have to be dead silence. Bluetooth will not save them.

An hour in the pool is an hour you cannot spend on a podcast, an audiobook, or the album you meant to finish. For some people that is the point. For a lot of people it is the reason they skip the swim.

Audio does not make you faster. It makes the session something other than counting tiles. Same body of water. Different hour.

Why your current headphones fail here

AirPods and most consumer buds are not a swim product. Some will survive a splash. Very few are meant to be submerged on purpose, and none of them solve the actual problem, which is radio.

Bluetooth does not travel through water. That is physics, not a firmware note. Water absorbs the 2.4 GHz band almost immediately. If the phone is on the deck and your head is in the lane, the stream dies. If you put the phone in a float, you are still dragging a radio through a medium it cannot use.

Waterproof cases, IPX7 claims, “gym and rain” ratings — those describe whether the device survives wet. They do not describe whether a wireless signal reaches you at a metre and a half of depth. Survival and reception are different jobs.

What actually works underwater

Storage on the headset. Files you loaded at home. Playback that does not ask the deck for permission every few seconds.

That is why swim-specific bone-conduction models exist. They keep MP3s onboard, sit outside the canal, and keep the ear open so you can still hear a lifeguard, a turn, or another body in the lane. Open-ear is not a lifestyle feature in a pool. It is how you notice the wall.

Keep the volume where you can still hear yourself and the room. A mix that seals you off is a safety problem, not a preference.

Bone conduction, not in-ear

In-ear tips want a seal. A seal in the pool means water pressure, trapped water after the set, and a canal you now have to dry. It also means you hear less of the lane.

Bone conduction sits just in front of the ear and moves sound through bone. Nothing in the canal. You will not get isolation-can fidelity. You will get a voice and a beat you can follow for forty minutes without redesigning your ears.

The honest comparison

Other bone-conduction swim headphones already exist. Shokz OpenSwim is the name most people are told to buy, and for a pool-only life it is often the correct recommendation: MP3 storage, open ear, built for water. This category is not empty.

What those products are often not is a sauna product. Many are explicit about heat. A pool is ~27°C. A steam room is 40–45°C and 100% humidity. A traditional sauna is 80–100°C. Those are different materials problems. Silicone that is happy in a lane will still soften, slip and die on a hot bench. Frames warp. Batteries that were specified for sport were not specified for 110°C.

If you only swim, buy a swim headset. If you also sit in heat, or you contrast swim with steam, or you want one pair that walks from the pool to the plunge to the stack, you need the temperature range too. That is the gap Dipsana is built for — not a claim that swim audio was unsolved.

How to set it up

Load the files on dry land. Podcasts, chapters, a playlist with a known length. 32GB is a lot of speech. You do not need to stand at the locker pairing anything with wet hands.

Above water — deck, warmup, walk between rooms — Bluetooth 5.4 is the convenient path. Below water, the headset has to forget the phone. Offline MP3 is the mode that matches the physics.

Physical buttons matter once your fingers are cold or pruney. Touch surfaces do not. Magnetic charging with no port matters after, because ports corrode. None of this is swimming-specific. It is just what water does to consumer electronics, slowly and then all at once.

Safety

Hear the lane. Hear other people. Hear yourself. If you are dizzy, tired, or off, get out. Audio can make a set useful. It cannot make a cramp optional, and it is not a substitute for knowing how to swim the distance you signed up for.
